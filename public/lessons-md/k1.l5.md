# K1.L5 — Traffic and Rollouts

_Track K1: Knative Serving · ~17 min · functionspace_

> A rollout in Knative is an edit to a table of percentages. The table can follow "latest" automatically, pin named revisions, give each one its own URL, or walk from 0 to 100% over sixty seconds on its own.
Everything in K1.L1 leads here. Because revisions are immutable and stay addressable, the Route's **traffic table** is the only thing that decides who serves. Four features of that table cover almost every rollout pattern:

1. **Percentages** across any set of revisions.
2. **`latestRevision: true`** — an entry that follows whatever revision is newest and Ready.
3. **Tags** — a stable per-revision URL, independent of the percentages.
4. **Rollout duration** — let Knative walk `latest` from 0 to 100% gradually.

---

## Named versus latest

A traffic entry either names a revision (`revisionName: alice-rollout-blue`) or says `latestRevision: true`. The difference decides what the *next* deploy does:

| entry | on the next `kn service update` |
|---|---|
| `latestRevision: true` (`@latest`) | moves to the new revision as soon as it is Ready |
| `revisionName: …` | stays exactly where it is; the new revision gets **0%** |

A plain `kn service create` writes one `@latest` entry at 100%. That is continuous deployment by default. The moment you care about controlled releases, replace it with named entries — then deploys become "create a dark revision", and releases become "edit the table". In lab 03 a third revision, `red`, was deployed while the table named blue and green; it got nothing.

---

```bash
kn service update $S --tag $S-blue=blue --tag $S-green=green
kn service update $S --traffic blue=80 --traffic green=20
kubectl get ksvc $S -o jsonpath='{.status.traffic}'
# [{"latestRevision":false,"percent":80,"revisionName":"alice-rollout-blue","tag":"blue",
#   "url":"http://blue-alice-rollout.default.127.0.0.1.sslip.io"},
#  {"latestRevision":false,"percent":20,"revisionName":"alice-rollout-green","tag":"green",
#   "url":"http://green-alice-rollout.default.127.0.0.1.sslip.io"}]
```

---

The split is per request and random. 200 requests came out **155 / 45** (77.5 / 22.5) on one run and **84 / 16** of 100 on another. If you need a user to see the same version on every request, a percentage split alone will not do it — use a tag URL, or put stickiness in front of Knative.

---

## Tags: test in production without exposing anyone

A tag gives a revision its own hostname: `<tag>-<service>.<namespace>.<domain>`. A tag can carry **0%** of the main traffic and still be reachable at its URL, which is the cleanest way to smoke-test a new revision on real infrastructure: deploy dark, tag it `candidate`, run your checks against `candidate-…`, then move percentages.

---

## Gradual rollout, built in

Set `serving.knative.dev/rollout-duration` on the Service and every time the `@latest` target changes, Knative walks traffic from the old revision to the new one over that duration instead of switching at once. Measured on the reference cluster with 60 s:

---

```text
t=5s   alice-hello-00004=93%  alice-hello-00005=7%
t=15s  alice-hello-00004=73%  alice-hello-00005=27%
t=30s  alice-hello-00004=45%  alice-hello-00005=55%
t=45s  alice-hello-00004=13%  alice-hello-00005=87%
t=70s  alice-hello-00005=100%
```

---

Roughly linear, as documented. It applies only to `@latest` targets — named targets never move by themselves — and it is **not** a canary *analysis*: nothing watches error rates and stops the rollout. If revision 00005 were returning errors, it would still reach 100% at 60 s. For automated rollback on metrics you still need something outside Knative that edits the table.

---

_fig 1 — four release patterns, one table_

Components: traffic table (Route status) · continuous (@latest = 100%) · blue / green (named 100 → 0) · canary (named 95 / 5) · gradual (rollout-duration)

Steps:
1. Every release pattern is just a way of writing the same table: a list of revisions, percentages and optional tags that the Route programs into Kourier.
2. Continuous deployment is the default: one @latest entry at 100%, so every Ready revision takes all traffic immediately.
3. Blue/green names two revisions and flips 100/0 to 0/100 in one edit — and back again, which is the rollback.
4. A canary names two revisions at 95/5, holds while someone watches the metrics, then moves the numbers by hand or by a tool.
5. A gradual rollout lets Knative move an @latest target over rollout-duration seconds — automatic, but blind to errors.

**Q1. Your team runs a 90/10 canary with named revisions. A developer ships a hotfix with kn service update. What traffic does the hotfix revision get?**
   A. 100% — updates always take all traffic
   B. 10% — it replaces the canary automatically
   C. 0% — named targets do not move; the hotfix is deployed dark until someone edits the table
   D. It is rejected until the canary finishes
   Answer: C — Only @latest entries follow new revisions. With a fully named table the new revision is Ready but unrouted — often a surprise during an incident. Your runbook should say how to route a hotfix (tag it, then move traffic) when the table is pinned.

**Q2. A product manager asks for "a gradual rollout that stops automatically if errors rise". Is rollout-duration enough?**
   A. Yes — it pauses on errors by default
   B. No — it moves traffic on a timer and does not look at errors; automatic halt needs a component outside Knative watching metrics and editing the traffic table
   C. Yes, if you also set min-scale
   D. No — Knative cannot do gradual rollouts at all
   Answer: B — rollout-duration is time-based traffic shifting, not analysis. Promising automatic halt on the strength of it is the kind of claim that fails in the first real incident. Name the missing piece in the design.

**Q3. QA wants to test the new revision in the production cluster without any customer traffic reaching it. What do you do?**
   A. Deploy to a separate namespace
   B. Deploy it dark with named traffic on the current revision, tag the new one, and point QA at its tag URL
   C. Set its traffic to 1% and hope
   D. Scale the current revision to zero during the test
   Answer: B — Tags give a 0%-traffic revision its own URL on the same routing layer, so QA tests exactly what will be promoted. 1% still exposes customers; a separate namespace is a different deployment.
